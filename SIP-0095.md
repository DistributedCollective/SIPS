# SIP-0095: Perimeter Withdrawal Delay (continuation of SIP-0094)

## Summary

SIP-0094 installed the Perimeter Fee, the funding rail of the Sovryn security perimeter, and said plainly that it was phase 1 of two: the rail first, the protection after. This is that protection. SIP-0095 installs the **Perimeter withdrawal delay** — a hold placed on the user leg of an exit, long enough that a theft detected while it is in flight can be stopped before the value leaves the perimeter.

It is voted as two technical proposals, both on the owner governor, split for the reason SIP-0094 was split: the governor caps any single proposal at ten actions (`proposalMaxOperations`), a split Bitocracy has used before (SIP-0046, Parts 1–4; SIP-0084, Parts 1–2; SIP-0094, Parts 1–3).

**Part 1** carries the lending half — ten actions, all of them code installation and pointer wiring on the lending protocol.
**Part 2** carries the Zero half — five actions: four across Zero's two exit-side proxies, and one unrelated implementation swap on the TroveManager proxy that rides this release because it upgrades the same product under the same governor.

There is no treasury leg and no third part. The Adoption Fund transfer and the Stability Pool subsidy retirement were one-time actions of SIP-0094 and are already executed; this release carries only what the delay adds.

Each part executes atomically within itself — there is no partial-installation state — and the two parts are **independent and self-contained**. Part 1 touches only the lending protocol; Part 2 touches only Zero's two proxies; neither depends on anything the other installs. Both depend on the Exchequer having already upgraded the perimeter controller to the delay build, and both builders refuse to construct a proposal until that holds — a check made when the proposal is created, not when it executes. Part 2 refuses on a second count as well: it reads the protocol's own perimeter wiring and will not build until SIP-0094's effect is present on chain. Part 1 carries no such check, and does not need one to be safe — it relies on the operator to sequence it after SIP-0094, which is the natural order, since the modules Part 1 installs are the delay vintage of the very modules SIP-0094 introduces.

Order of execution is therefore not a correctness question but a coverage one. If Part 2 executes first, Zero is delay-hooked while lending is not — inert while the delay is unarmed, and an asymmetry only if the delay were armed before both parts land. Arming waits for both; see *Operator steps*.

**Nothing is held when these proposals execute.** The queue is installed and pinned, but inert: the **delay switch** on the controller (`securityPerimeterEnabled`) reads false and the global delay is zero, so every delay quote answers zero and the queue is never touched. That switch is a different flag from SIP-0094's fee switch (`exitFeeEnabled`), and the two are independent in both directions: turning the delay on or off does not touch the Perimeter Fee, and turning the fee on or off does not touch the delay. Holding begins only when the Exchequer arms the delay after post-deployment verification — a separate step, after both parts execute, described under *Operator steps* below.

## Background

*This document is written for publication after SIP-0094 has executed and the Exchequer has taken its enable step; statements here about what the Perimeter Fee does today assume that state, and are re-read against chain before publication.*

The perimeter is a trust boundary drawn around the protocol's holdings. Inside it nothing changes; the boundary acts only where value leaves, because exits are where DeFi losses actually happen. SIP-0094 built the rail on that boundary at a deliberately minimal 0.10% on user-initiated withdrawals. A rail alone stops nothing. What it funds — and what this release installs — is the ability to intervene in the window between a suspicious withdrawal beginning and the value being gone.

The delay is that window, made explicit. On a hooked exit today, the user's leg is paid out in the same transaction that asks for it. With the delay armed, the user's leg is instead escrowed in a new contract, the **exit delay queue**, as an immutable request with its own identifier and its own unlock time. When the time passes, the rightful parties execute the request and are paid. Between the two moments, and only in that window, the protocol has something it has never had: a place to stand.

What the queue can do in that window, and nothing more:

- **Hold and release.** Every held request is executed by identifier, by the parties entitled to it. There is no ordering assumption and no cursor: a request that is ready is paid, whatever else is queued. A recipient whose address bounces the payment is a self-service case — the entitled parties redirect their own payout, and only after the original destination genuinely fails.
- **Block by address.** The fast guardian can *freeze* (reversible) or *blacklist* an address on the strength of a specific queued request. These are address states, not request states: blocking a party holds every request that party is entitled to, present and future, not only the one that triggered it.
- **Pause.** A single switch stops payouts to users: ordinary execution and the self-service redirect both refuse while it is set. Ingress stays live — new exits still arrive and are still held — and so do both recovery legs, which the pause does not gate. A pause is also one of the conditions that brings a held request within reach of the owner's catch-all leg, so setting it widens that leg to every request in the queue.
- **Recovery routes.** Where a theft is established, escrowed value can be sent back along a route the owner registered in advance — matched exactly on the request's surface, sub-product and token — or, as an owner catch-all, to a named destination. Both are bounded; see *Security considerations*.
- **Delay switch.** One controller flag turns the whole delay off: quotes answer zero and exits pass straight through. It is the instrument for a fault in the delay machinery itself, reachable in minutes, and it is independent of SIP-0094's fee switch in both directions.

The queue is deployed by the Exchequer Multisig from the perimeter repository and is owned and administered by it, on the same two-role split SIP-0094 established for the fee contracts. The owner role holds upgrades, the delay length, the registration of recovery routes, the marking of a surface as eligible for a refund back into its own pool, and the catch-all recovery leg. The fast operational role holds freezing, blacklisting, the pause, the delay switch — **and** the execution of a recovery along a route the owner has already registered. That last power is the one to read twice: the fast role can move escrowed value, but only for a request whose party it has blacklisted and only to a destination the owner registered in advance. Route registration is what bounds it. Neither role can reach a user's deposits; both can only act on value that is already, and visibly, inside the queue.

## Motivation

SIP-0094 asked holders to pay for a service on the promise that the service would follow. This is the delivery of that promise, and it is the part that changes the arithmetic of an exploit against Sovryn. Today, and every day until this ships, a bug becomes an irreversible withdrawal at the speed of a block. With the delay armed, the same withdrawal is visible, escrowed and reversible for the length of the window — which turns an attack from a race the protocol always loses into one it can win with ordinary human response time.

The cost is honest and should be stated as plainly as the benefit: a user's withdrawal no longer completes in one transaction. It completes in two, separated by the delay, and the interface shows the unlock time before the first is confirmed. That is a real change to how Sovryn feels to use, and it is the whole product. The rate schedule that SIP-0094 said would publish with phase 2 publishes alongside this proposal, so the fee and the protection it buys are voted on together.

## Specification

Both parts run on **GovernorOwner**, which owns every contract they touch. Every action carries a value of zero — no RBTC moves in either proposal. Addresses marked `<TBD — filled at deployment>` are contracts deployed before the proposals are created; they are filled in, and source-verified on the Rootstock Blockscout explorer, before this document is published and before any vote opens.

**Precondition, and it is not optional.** Before Part 1 is created, the Exchequer must upgrade the ExitFeeController proxy to the delay build. The modules Part 1 installs quote a hold on every hooked exit and **fail closed** when the controller cannot answer that quote; run against the controller build that predates the delay, Part 1 would revert every hooked withdrawal the moment it executed. The proposal builders enforce this: they refuse to build either part against a controller that does not answer both delay views. See *Operator steps*.

**The order of the first four is load-bearing, in both halves.** The pool comes first because the delay build of the borrower contract settles a surplus claim through a pool entry point the live pool does not have, and there is no fallback around that call — a borrower contract installed ahead of its pool would leave the surplus claim reverting. Actions 3 and 4 must then immediately follow action 2 in the same transaction, because those two setters exist only on the implementation action 2 installs; without them in the same transaction the hooked contract would be live with an unset settlement companion or an unset queue.

The builder decides whether the pool is upgraded by comparing deployed runtime code rather than addresses, and would omit action 1 if the delay build ever proved byte-identical to the implementation the proxy serves today. It does not: the delay build adds the surplus-claim entry point the settlement companion uses whenever a fee or a delay applies (falling back to the original claim path when neither does), and the presence of that entry point in the source is by itself enough to change the compiled bytecode. The comparison therefore always finds the pool changed, and the perimeter half of Part 2 is four actions.

**Action 5 is not perimeter work.** Zero forked Liquity before Liquity corrected a defect in its own launch code, and Zero still carries it. In Recovery Mode, when several troves are liquidated in one call, the contract keeps a running total of system collateral in order to re-judge the collateral ratio before each trove in the batch. That running total omitted two amounts that leave the system with every liquidation: the liquidator's gas compensation, and the borrower's collateral surplus. After a capped liquidation the next trove was therefore judged against a ratio that was too high — so a trove above the real threshold could be liquidated, its owner losing roughly a tenth of the debt value, or the batch could conclude the system had left Recovery Mode one trove early and stop. `batchLiquidateTroves` is the call liquidation bots make. Liquity corrected this in its repository in 2021, after its own immutable deployment; the correction is nine lines across the two loops, and this action installs it.

It rides this release rather than waiting for one of its own because it upgrades the same product through the same governor, and it is placed last because nothing the perimeter installs depends on it. It adds no state, so the storage behind the TroveManager proxy is unchanged, and it re-wires nothing: the implementation is address-compatible and the proxy's pointers survive the swap. Its two constructor arguments are immutable and are therefore part of the deployed code; the builder refuses to construct this action unless both match what the proxy serves today, so the upgrade cannot silently move the redemption bootstrap window. Liquity's own regression suite for the defect has been ported to Zero and is part of the release evidence: it fails on the current implementation and passes on this one.

There is no controller action in this part. The controller pointer both products carry is already live from SIP-0094, and it survives the implementation swap because it lives in a fixed storage slot rather than in the implementation. Part 2 therefore **asserts** that pointer instead of rewriting it, so a chain that never received SIP-0094 fails at proposal creation rather than quietly installing a delay over nothing.

## Operator steps

Five things happen around these proposals that are not proposal actions. They are Exchequer Multisig transactions on Exchequer-owned contracts (`0x924f5ad34698Fd20c90Fe5D5A8A0abd3b42dc711`), listed here so this document describes the whole release in one place.

### Before Part 1 is created

1. **Deploy the delay build.** The delay-vintage implementations named in the tables above, and the `ExitDelayQueue` itself, are deployed from the perimeter repository and source-verified on the Rootstock Blockscout explorer. The queue's owner and its fast operational role are both the Exchequer Multisig at launch, on the same split SIP-0094 established for the fee contracts.
2. **Register every hooked source on the queue.** Only a registered source may record an exit, and the list is exact: the lending protocol singleton `0x5A0D867e0D70Fcc6Ade25C3F1B89d618b5B4Eaa7`; **each** hooked lending pool, because every iToken records its own lenders' exits under its own address and is therefore its own source; and Zero's BorrowerOperations proxy `0x5B9dB4B8bdeF3e57323187a9AC2639C5DEe5FD39`. Zero's active pool is deliberately not on the list — it only pushes native RBTC into the queue, and the record that accompanies that push comes from BorrowerOperations. Sources are registered at the queue's initialisation or by owner calls afterwards, and the list must be complete before the delay is armed: an omitted source is fail-closed, so every withdrawal through it would revert rather than pass unheld.
3. **Upgrade the controller.** The Exchequer upgrades the ExitFeeController proxy `0x99994b4522483de17f31a5bc010c5901add3440e` to the delay build. This is the precondition stated in the specification, and it is checked mechanically: both proposal builders read the controller's two delay views and refuse to build a proposal against a proxy that does not answer them. The upgrade changes no behaviour on its own: the delay it adds arrives switched off, and the Perimeter Fee is left exactly as SIP-0094's own enable step leaves it.

### After Part 2 executes

4. **Arm the delay.** Two settings, in this order. First the global delay length, `setGlobalDelaySeconds`, which is owner-only, set to `<TBD — filled at deployment>`; it must be at least the per-request floor the queue enforces, or every non-bypassed exit would refuse itself. Then the delay switch, `setSecurityPerimeterEnabled(true)`, which the fast operational role can also reach so that it can be flipped back inside minutes. Until both are set, every delay quote answers zero and the queue is never touched. This step waits on post-deployment verification, live monitoring, and a rehearsed drill of the pause and the delay switch — the same bar SIP-0094 set for its enable step. It changes nothing about the fee.
5. **Register recovery routes, if any.** A route that refunds escrowed value back into the pool it came from requires the owner to mark that surface eligible for a pool top-up **before** the route is registered; the registration is refused otherwise. Route registration is owner-only, and every registration emits a public event.

## Rollback

Every action in both parts is reversible by the authority that made it, and the anchors are recorded before the vote: for each replaced module and each replaced implementation, the currently-live address it replaces is listed in *Deployed contracts and rollback anchors* below.

- **Lending beacon modules (Part 1, actions 1–2).** A later owner-governor proposal re-registers the previous module on each beacon. Beacon registration reads the module's own selector list and de-registers selectors the module has dropped, so a beacon rollback is symmetric: the same action with the older address restores the older routing exactly.
- **Protocol modules (Part 1, actions 3–8).** The same `replaceContract` call re-installs the currently-live module. **This is not symmetric**, and a rollback proposal must account for it: `replaceContract` only *adds* routes for the selectors the module it installs declares — it never removes ones the old module declared and the new one does not. Rolling the protocol back to the fee-vintage perimeter administration module therefore leaves `setExitDelayQueue(address)` still routed to the delay-vintage module, which stays deployed. A complete rollback pairs the module replacement with an explicit `setTargets(["setExitDelayQueue(address)"], [0x0])` to retire that route.
- **Protocol pointers (Part 1, actions 9–10).** The borrower settlement companion and the queue pointer are re-pointed by the same owner setters this proposal uses. The rollback anchor for action 9 is the settlement companion the protocol points at today.
- **Zero (Part 2).** Each proxy is set back to the implementation it serves today by the same `setImplementation` call — in the mirror order, the borrower contract before the pool — and the two pointer setters are re-run against whatever that implementation expects.
- **The controller.** The fee build the proxy serves today, implementation `0x50ec5c1c156cfa7e3007a0b0c97298e4f58a552d`, remains the rollback target for the controller upgrade: the Exchequer can put the proxy back on it, which removes the delay machinery from the controller entirely and leaves the Perimeter Fee behaving exactly as SIP-0094 shipped it. Roll the controller back only together with the product modules, never underneath them: the delay build of the lending modules fails closed against a controller that cannot answer a delay quote.
- **Faster than any of the above.** Setting the delay switch (`setSecurityPerimeterEnabled`) to false, or setting the global delay to zero, stops all new holds without changing a single line of installed code. Neither touches the Perimeter Fee, which is left on its own switch exactly as SIP-0094 leaves it; neither releases, redirects or otherwise reaches what the queue already holds. Both are Exchequer transactions, effective in minutes, and neither needs a vote. That is the instrument for an incident; the rollback actions above are the instrument for a decision.

## Security considerations

**The delay leg fails closed; the fee leg fails open, and that difference is deliberate.** SIP-0094's guarantee was that no fault in the fee machinery could shrink or block a user's withdrawal — on any fault the protocol forgoes its fee and pays the user in full. The delay cannot inherit that rule, because a hold that silently does not happen is precisely the failure the perimeter exists to prevent. So a hooked module that cannot get an answer from the controller reverts the withdrawal rather than paying it out unheld. The user-visible consequence is real and worth stating: with the delay installed, a fault in the perimeter can stop a withdrawal from starting. It can never stop one from finishing — value already escrowed is paid to its rightful parties on unlock through a path that does not consult the controller at all. The fee leg's fail-open behaviour is unchanged by this release.

This asymmetry is the whole reason the controller upgrade is a precondition rather than a follow-up, and why the builders refuse to construct a proposal until it has happened.

**The pause is a lockable door, not a shutter — and it opens a second door as it closes the first.** Pausing stops payouts to users: ordinary execution refuses, and so does the self-service redirect that an entitled party would otherwise use when their own destination bounces. Ingress is untouched, so exits still arrive and are still held. That is the correct shape for an incident — the last thing wanted while an exploit is being triaged is a queue that has stopped accepting the very withdrawals under suspicion — but two consequences follow and both should be voted on knowingly. First, a pause held open keeps accumulating custody while giving no one a way out, including honest users with a bouncing recipient. Second, the two recovery legs are *not* pause-gated, and an active pause is itself one of the conditions that puts a request within reach of the owner's catch-all leg: while the queue is paused, every held request is reachable by that leg, not merely the blocked or still-locked ones. Pausing therefore widens the owner's discretionary reach over the whole queue for as long as it is set. It is a guardian-speed lever meant for hours, released by the same role that set it.

**Blocking is by address, and it holds everything that address is entitled to.** A freeze or a blacklist is raised from a specific queued request, but it lands on the party — so every request that party originated or owns is held, including ones that arrive afterwards. This is intended: an attacker draining through many small exits is stopped once, not once per request. It also means a mistaken block is broader than the request that prompted it. Both states are reversible by the same fast role that set them. What makes a blacklist the heavier of the two is not permanence but authority: it is the state that unlocks route-based recovery, and a freeze does not.

**Refunds are gated behind a blacklist, and the fast role can execute them.** Escrowed value can be sent back along a registered route only once the originator or the owner of the request is *blacklisted* — a freeze alone will not do it, a block on the receiver alone will not do it, and a batch that mixes qualifying and non-qualifying requests is refused wholesale rather than partly applied. Each request must match the route exactly on its surface, its sub-product and its token. Registering a route is owner-only; *executing* one is available to the fast operational role, which is the disclosure to weigh: that role can, on its own authority, blacklist a party and move that party's escrowed value — but only to a destination the owner had already registered for exactly that surface, sub-product and token. Route registration is the whole of the bound, which is why it is owner-only and why every registration is public. A refund-to-pool route is narrower still: it is available only on surfaces the owner has separately marked eligible, and it must name the very pool the exit came from as its destination, so the top-up label cannot be used to carry value elsewhere. The owner's catch-all leg is bounded differently — by request state rather than by route — reaching only requests that are blocked, paused, or still inside their delay window. A request past its unlock time with no party blocked and the queue unpaused is out of reach of every recovery path; it belongs to its owner.

**The delay switch makes the queue pass-through; it does not empty it.** Setting `securityPerimeterEnabled` to false makes every delay quote answer zero, so new exits pay straight through as they do today. It does not release, cancel, redirect or reach into what the queue already holds: those requests keep their own unlock times and are executed by their rightful parties exactly as before. Turning the delay off is therefore not a way to flush the queue, and not a way to strand it either. It is also not a way to stop the Perimeter Fee: the two switches are separate flags and independent in both directions, so the fee keeps behaving exactly as SIP-0094 left it whichever way this one is set. The delay switch is reachable by the fast operational role in both directions, for sub-minute response, and the accepted residual is stated openly: that role can switch the protection **off**, which is a fail-open loss of protection, and cannot use it to take or divert anything.

**New custody, disclosed.** For the length of the delay window, the user leg of a hooked exit sits in the queue rather than in the user's wallet. That is new contract risk and new custody, mitigated the way the rest of the perimeter is: the escrow is per-request and immutable once recorded, solvency against the sum of held requests is checked on every payout, the only ways out are the four bounded legs described above, and each of them emits a public event the moment it is used. As with SIP-0094, the roles that can act are the Exchequer's, deposits in the products themselves are reachable by neither role, and every configuration change is visible on chain the instant it is made.
